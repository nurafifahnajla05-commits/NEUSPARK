🎨 Colour Key:
Frontal Parietal Temporal Occipital Cerebellum Brainstem

🧠 Frontal Lobe

Controls decision-making, planning, personality, voluntary movement, and problem-solving. It's the brain's "CEO" — responsible for impulse control and long-term thinking.

🟣 Parietal Lobe

Processes sensory information from the body — touch, temperature, pain. Also integrates spatial awareness, helping you navigate and understand where your body is in space.

🟡 Temporal Lobe

Handles hearing, language comprehension, and memory formation. The hippocampus, located here, is essential for converting short-term memories into long-term storage.

🔴 Occipital Lobe

The brain's visual processing centre. It interprets colour, shape, motion, and depth from signals sent by your eyes — making it possible to "see" and understand images.

🟢 Cerebellum

Coordinates movement, balance, and fine motor control. It also plays roles in timing, language, and some aspects of emotional processing.

🟠 Brainstem

Controls automatic life-sustaining functions: breathing, heart rate, swallowing, and sleep-wake cycles. It connects the brain to the spinal cord and the rest of the body.

Disorders (SLDs)

Dyslexia, Dyscalculia, and Dysgraphia are the three most common specific learning disorders. They are brain-based — not caused by low intelligence or lack of effort.

Specific Learning Disorder

What are Specific Learning Disorders?

Specific Learning Disorders (SLDs) are neurodevelopmental conditions that affect the brain's ability to process and use certain types of information. They typically emerge during school years and can persist into adulthood.

People with SLDs often have average or above-average intelligence — the challenge lies in how their brains process specific inputs like written words, numbers, or motor sequences for writing.

Reading & Language

Dyslexia

Dyslexia is a learning disorder that affects reading, spelling, and decoding of written language. It is the most common specific learning disorder, caused by differences in how the brain processes phonological (sound-based) information.

🔬

Brain Mechanisms

  • Left Temporal-Parietal Cortex: reduced activation during phonological processing — difficulty mapping letters to sounds
  • Broca's Area (Frontal Lobe): underactivation during reading tasks, affecting language articulation
  • Angular Gyrus: poor cross-modal integration between visual symbols and sounds
  • Visual Word Form Area: slower processing of written words as whole units
🧩

Cognitive Impact

  • Difficulty decoding unfamiliar words phonetically
  • Slow and effortful reading with frequent errors
  • Poor spelling despite repeated practice
  • Difficulty with rhyming and sound manipulation
  • Trouble remembering sequences (e.g., alphabet, days of week)
🌀

Behavioural Consequences

  • Avoidance of reading aloud in class
  • Poor academic performance despite effort
  • Low self-esteem and frustration with schoolwork
  • Difficulty following written instructions
  • May appear "lazy" — often misunderstood by teachers
🔢
Numbers & Maths

Dyscalculia

Dyscalculia is a learning disorder that affects a person's ability to understand numbers, learn arithmetic facts, and perform mathematical calculations. It affects approximately 3–7% of school-age children.

🔬

Brain Mechanisms

  • Intraparietal Sulcus (Parietal Lobe): reduced grey matter and activation — this region is the core "number sense" area
  • Prefrontal Cortex: working memory deficits affect holding and manipulating numbers mentally
  • Right Hemisphere: spatial reasoning impairments affect understanding of number lines and geometry
  • Hippocampus: difficulty retrieving stored arithmetic facts from memory
🧩

Cognitive Impact

  • Weak number sense — difficulty estimating quantities
  • Trouble memorising multiplication tables
  • Poor understanding of place value and fractions
  • Difficulty telling time and managing money
  • Relies on finger-counting beyond expected age
🌀

Behavioural Consequences

  • Anxiety and avoidance during maths lessons
  • Difficulty managing daily tasks (budgeting, schedules)
  • Struggles with subjects that rely on maths (physics, chemistry)
  • Low confidence in academic abilities
  • May take significantly longer to complete maths exams
✏️
Writing & Motor

Dysgraphia

Dysgraphia is a learning disorder characterised by difficulty with handwriting, spelling, and putting thoughts into written form. It involves impairments in the fine motor and language systems of the brain.

🔬

Brain Mechanisms

  • Motor Cortex (Frontal Lobe): difficulty coordinating the fine motor sequences required for handwriting
  • Cerebellum: impaired motor learning and automation of writing movements
  • Parietal Lobe: spatial processing deficits affect letter sizing, spacing, and alignment
  • Basal Ganglia: difficulty automating learned motor patterns for letter formation
🧩

Cognitive Impact

  • Illegible or inconsistent handwriting
  • Difficulty organising thoughts into written form
  • Slow writing speed — cannot keep up in class
  • Mixing upper and lower case letters randomly
  • Omitting words or letters when writing sentences
🌀

Behavioural Consequences

  • Avoiding writing tasks or submitting incomplete work
  • Hand fatigue and pain during prolonged writing
  • Frustration — thoughts are clear but hard to express in writing
  • Poor exam performance despite knowing the content
  • May appear disorganised or careless to teachers
💡 Did You Know? All three disorders — Dyslexia, Dyscalculia, and Dysgraphia — can co-occur in the same individual. Early identification, specialised teaching strategies, assistive technology, and classroom accommodations can significantly improve outcomes for students with SLDs.

Are These Circles Moving?

Stare at the centre dot without moving your eyes. The rings appear to rotate — but they are completely still.

Your brain's motion-detection neurons fire due to high-contrast colour patterns and peripheral vision processing. The occipital lobe interprets edge contrast differences as motion signals — a classic example of peripheral drift illusion.

Same Colour or Different?

Squares A and B appear to be different shades — one dark, one light. They are actually the exact same colour.

The brain uses colour constancy — it adjusts perceived colour based on surrounding context. The shadow cast over Square B makes your brain compensate, lightening its perceived shade. This is processed in the V4 area of the visual cortex.

Which Line is Longer?

Line A looks shorter than Line B — but they are identical in length.

This is the Müller-Lyer illusion. Arrow fins pointing inward make the line appear shorter; outward fins make it appear longer. The brain uses these cues as depth signals — similar to how corners of buildings look farther or closer away.

Face or Vase?

Look at the image. Do you see a vase in the centre, or two faces looking at each other?

This is Rubin's Vase. Your brain cannot hold both interpretations simultaneously — it switches between them. This is called perceptual rivalry. The right hemisphere tends to process faces, while the left processes shapes. They "compete" for dominance.
